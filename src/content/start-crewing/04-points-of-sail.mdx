A sailing boat can travel in almost any direction — except straight into the wind. The angle between the boat and
the wind is called its **point of sail**, and each one has a name.

## The no-go zone

About **45° either side of the wind** is the no-go zone. Point the boat in there and the sails just flap — you’ll
slow down and stop. A boat stuck pointing into the wind is said to be **“in irons”**.

To get upwind, sailors **zig-zag**: close-hauled on one tack, then tack, then close-hauled on the other. That’s
called **beating** to windward.

## The points of sail

| Point of sail | Angle to the wind | Sails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Close-hauled** | ~45° | Pulled in hard |
| **Close reach** | ~60–80° | Eased a little |
| **Beam reach** | ~90° (wind on the side) | Eased about halfway |
| **Broad reach** | ~100–160° (wind over the back corner) | Eased well out |
| **Run** | ~180° (wind from behind) | All the way out |

<Callout type="remember" title="The golden rule of trim">
  The closer you sail to the wind, the more you pull the sails in. The further you turn away from the wind, the more
  you let them out. “If in doubt, let it out” — then pull in until the sail just stops flapping.
</Callout>

## Port tack and starboard tack

The **tack** describes which side the wind is coming over:

- Wind over the **starboard** (right) side → **starboard tack** → the boom is out to port.
- Wind over the **port** (left) side → **port tack** → the boom is out to starboard.

This matters a lot — it decides who has right of way. You’ll meet that in Start Skippering and Start Racing.

## Heading up and bearing away

- **Head up** (or “luff up”): turn **towards** the wind. Sheet in as you go.
- **Bear away**: turn **away** from the wind. Ease the sheets as you go.
