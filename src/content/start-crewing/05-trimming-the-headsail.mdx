As crew, your main job between manoeuvres is usually the **jib** (or genoa). A well-trimmed jib makes the boat
faster, point higher and feel balanced.

## How a sail works

A sail is a **wing**. When air flows smoothly across both sides of the curved sail, it creates lift that pulls the
boat along — just like an aeroplane wing. Two things ruin it:

- **Luffing** — the sail is too far out (or the boat is pointing too close to the wind). Air hits the back of the
  sail’s front edge and it flaps. No power.
- **Stalling** — the sail is pulled in too tight. Air can’t follow the curve on the leeward side, breaks away and
  swirls. Lots of heel, little speed.

## Telltales tell you everything

Short pieces of wool near the front edge (luff) of the jib, on both sides, show the airflow:

| What you see | What it means | Trim | Or steer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Both streaming aft | Perfect | Hold it | Hold course |
| **Windward** telltale lifting | Sail too eased (luffing) | **Sheet in** | Bear away |
| **Leeward** telltale lifting | Sail too tight (stalled) | **Ease** | Head up |

<Callout type="remember" title="Memory hook">
  “Windward lifts — sheet in. Leeward lifts — let it out.” You can see the windward telltale through the sail, or
  through a little window if the sail has one.
</Callout>

## The trimming routine

1. **Ease** the sheet until the front of the sail just starts to flap (or the windward telltale lifts).
2. **Trim** in just until it stops.
3. Repeat every time the course or the wind changes. Good trimmers never stop trimming.

## Other controls you may meet

- **Jib cars (fairleads)** — slide forward to make the sail fuller (light winds, waves); back to flatten it (strong winds).
- **Halyard tension** — tighter in a breeze, a little softer in light air.
- **Furler** — on many keelboats the jib rolls up around the forestay. Partly rolling it is a quick way to reduce power.

  Keep fingers clear of the winch and fairleads when the sheet is loaded. The next lesson covers winches properly.
