On a boat, things happen quickly. When the skipper calls *“ease the jib sheet”* or *“watch the boom”*, you
need to know exactly what they mean. That’s why sailing has its own language — it’s short, precise and works
over the noise of wind and water.

## What is a keelboat?

A keelboat has a heavy **keel** — a fin under the hull with lead in it. The keel does two jobs:

- **Stops the boat sliding sideways.** Its shape grips the water like a wing, so the wind's push turns into forward motion.
- **Keeps the boat upright.** The weight acts like a pendulum. However far a keelboat heels, the keel pulls it back up. Unlike a dinghy, it won’t capsize in normal sailing.

<Callout type="remember" title="Heeling is normal">
  When the wind blows, a keelboat leans over (heels). It can feel dramatic the first time, but it’s exactly what the
  boat is designed to do. Ease the sails and it stands back up.
</Callout>

## Sails and the ropes that control them

There are hardly any “ropes” on a boat — each one has a name that says what it does:

| Name | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Halyard** | Hoists (raises) a sail. |
| **Sheet** | Trims (pulls in or lets out) a sail. Jib sheet, mainsheet. |
| **Line** | General word for a rope with a job, e.g. mooring line. |
| **Outhaul, cunningham, vang** | Fine-tune the shape of the mainsail. |

<Callout type="tip">
  “Ease” means let out a little, under control. “Trim” or “sheet in” means pull in. “Let go” or “release” means let it run
  completely. Ask if you’re ever unsure — every skipper prefers a question to a mistake.
</Callout>

## Spars and rigging

- **Spars** are the poles: the **mast** (vertical) and the **boom** (horizontal, along the bottom of the mainsail).
- **Standing rigging** — the wires that hold the mast up: the **forestay** (front), **backstay** (back) and **shrouds** (sides).
- **Running rigging** — all the ropes that move: halyards, sheets and control lines.
