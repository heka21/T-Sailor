Taking the helm for the first time feels strange: push the tiller one way and the boat turns the other. Once you
understand why, it becomes second nature.

## Why it works “backwards”

The tiller is a lever attached to the top of the **rudder**. Moving the tiller turns the rudder blade the other
way. Water flowing past the blade pushes the **stern** sideways, and the boat pivots — so the **bow turns away from
the direction you push**.

<Callout type="remember">
  Push the tiller <strong>away</strong> from you (to leeward) → the boat turns <strong>towards</strong> you, into the
  wind (head up). Pull the tiller <strong>towards</strong> you (to windward) → the boat turns away from the wind (bear away).
</Callout>

## Wheel steering

Bigger keelboats have a wheel instead. A wheel works like a car: turn it right, the bow goes right. If you swap
between boats, take a moment to think before you steer.

## Holding the helm

- **Sit on the windward side**, facing forward, so you can see the sails, the telltales and the water ahead.
- Hold the **tiller extension** in your back hand with a light grip (dagger grip or pen grip) — small, smooth movements.
- Keep the mainsheet in your forward hand on smaller keelboats, if you’re also trimming the main.

## Small and smooth

Every time the rudder turns it also **slows the boat** — a rudder hard over acts like a brake. Good helms make small
corrections early instead of big corrections late.

## Steer to a target

Pick a landmark on the shore — a building, a tree, a navigation mark — and keep the bow pointed at it. Check the sails
often and adjust as the wind changes.

## Weather helm

If the boat keeps trying to turn into the wind and you need to hold the tiller to windward, that’s **weather helm**.
A little is good (the boat is safe — let go and it heads up and stops). A lot means the boat is overpowered: **ease the
mainsheet** or flatten the sails.
